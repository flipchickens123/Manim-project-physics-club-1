# Projectile Motion: narration script

> How this file works: each "## section" is spoken during the matching part of
> the animation in scenes/projectile.py, and the animation stretches to fit the
> words. Edit the wording freely, then re-render with `python render.py projectile`.
> Write numbers and symbols the way they should be SAID ("forty-five degrees",
> "H S E dot physics"). Lines starting with ">" are notes and are not spoken.

## intro
> Title card, then the empty graph appears.
Throw a ball, kick a football, or launch a water rocket, and it flies along the same kind of curve.
So which launch angle sends it the farthest?

## launch
> One ball appears with its two speed arrows.
Let's launch a ball at twenty metres per second, at fifty degrees.

## rise
> The ball climbs to the top of its path.
The blue arrow is its horizontal speed. The pink arrow is its vertical speed.
Gravity shrinks the pink arrow, until it hits zero at the very top.

## fall
> The ball comes back down.
Then gravity speeds it up again on the way down.
But the blue arrow never changes at all.

## idea
> The two summary lines appear.
That's the big idea. Sideways motion and up-and-down motion are completely independent.
Gravity only pulls down.

## angles
> Five balls launch at once.
Now let's launch five balls at the same speed: at fifteen, thirty, forty-five, sixty, and seventy-five degrees.
Which one lands the farthest?

## winner
> The 45 degree path is highlighted, then the 30 / 60 tie is marked.
Forty-five degrees wins, landing about forty-one metres away.
And look: thirty and sixty degrees land in exactly the same spot!

## formula
> The range formula appears.
Here's why. The range equals v squared, times sine of two theta, divided by g.
Sine is biggest at ninety degrees. So two theta is ninety, and theta is forty-five.

## outro
> Key idea card and follow prompt.
So on flat ground, with no air resistance, forty-five degrees always goes the farthest.
Follow H S E dot physics for more!
